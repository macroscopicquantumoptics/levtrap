Start here — what does this page do?

The trapping lens is placed in the vacuum chamber by hand, so it is never perfectly square to the beam. A small tilt moves the focus (sideways and along the beam) and adds aberrations — mainly coma and astigmatism — that smear the focal spot. That changes where the particle sits, how stiff the trap is, and whether it holds at all.

This page traces real rays through the actual asphere surfaces (from the Thorlabs Zemax file), turns the resulting aberrated wavefront into a focused beam, and computes the full optical force on the particle with the same Mie/T-matrix engine as the trap simulator. Because the lens was designed for 830 nm, using it at 1550 nm already adds some spherical aberration and moves the focus away from the lens — try the wavelength field to see this.

  1. Set the tilt (a few tenths of a degree is realistic for hand placement) and the beam diameter: a larger beam fills more of the lens (higher NA, stiffer trap) but is more sensitive to tilt, and overfilling clips light at the lens edge.
  2. Read the summary: how far the focus and the particle move, the three trap frequencies compared with a perfectly aligned lens, and whether the trap is still stable.
  3. Use the sweep at the bottom to see how everything changes with tilt angle or beam size.

Wavefront error across the lens

What am I looking at?

How far (in wavelengths) each part of the beam lags or leads a perfect spherical wave converging on the best focus, as seen across the input beam (the lens aperture). A perfect focus is flat (all zero). A tilt produces a characteristic coma pattern (odd, one side up and the other down); spherical aberration is a ring pattern. The rule of thumb (Maréchal): rms error below λ/14 ≈ 0.07 waves is “diffraction limited”, i.e. the focus is essentially perfect.

Strehl ratio ≈ exp[−(2π σW)²]    σW = rms wavefront error in waves

What am I looking at?

Each point re-traces the lens and recomputes the trap with one setting changed. Displacements are measured from the aligned lens. The sweep follows the particle: each point starts from where the particle sat at the previous one, as if you turned the lens slowly. “Jump” marks where that equilibrium disappears and the particle would hop to another one (or be lost). Grey bands: no stable trap. Each point takes a few seconds, so start with ~10 steps.

Model and limitations
  • Collimated Gaussian input beam along z; the lens is rotated about the chosen pivot on its axis (tilt about x first, then y) and optionally decentred.
  • Real ray trace through both even-asphere surfaces with Snell’s law; rays outside either clear aperture are blocked (this is the overfilling loss). Glass index: CDGM D-ZK3 Sellmeier formula with a constant offset (−0.0027) chosen so the trace reproduces the Zemax back focal distance (1.760 mm) at 830 nm; the lens is then diffraction-limited at 830 nm, as designed.
  • The aberrated wavefront behind the lens is expanded in vector spherical waves about the best focus (same basis as the trap simulator). Polarisation changes at the lens surfaces and Fresnel losses (the C coating) are neglected.
  • Forces: full Mie theory for a sphere in vacuum; equilibrium by Newton iteration on the 3D force, frequencies from the eigenvalues of the 3×3 stiffness matrix (with a tilted lens the trap axes rotate slightly).
  • Not included: the vacuum window or any other optics, mechanical clipping by the mount, thermal lensing, gravity.
