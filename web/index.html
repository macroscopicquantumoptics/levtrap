Start here — what is this?

In levitated optomechanics a glass or crystal sphere, a few hundred nanometres across, is held in vacuum by nothing but a focused laser beam (“optical tweezers”). Light carries momentum, so when it is bent or scattered by the sphere it pushes on it. Near a tight focus these pushes add up to a restoring force, and the sphere sits in a light-made “bowl” and oscillates in it like a mass on a spring.

This page calculates those forces exactly: it solves Maxwell’s equations for a sphere in a focused beam (Mie theory, T-matrix method from the Optical Tweezers Toolbox), entirely inside your browser.

  1. Pick a preset on the left (a standard silica “bright” trap, or a WS₂ particle in a “dark” bottle-beam trap).
  2. Change anything: particle size and material, laser wavelength, power and focusing. The results update automatically after a short pause.
  3. Read the summary: is the particle trapped, where it sits, how fast it oscillates and how deep the trap is. Hover over or tap any ⓘ for a plain-language explanation. Each plot has a “What am I looking at?” note, and there is a glossary at the bottom of the page.

Summary

Force through the trap

What am I looking at?

Each panel moves the sphere along one axis through the trap (x and y at the axial equilibrium height zeq; z along the beam axis) and plots the optical force component along that axis. A stable trap needs the force to point back towards the equilibrium on every axis: the curve crosses zero with a negative slope. The slope there is the spring constant, and the area under the restoring part is the well depth. The beam propagates towards +z, so radiation pressure pushes the equilibrium slightly downstream of the focus.

F = Q · nm P / c    κ = −∂F/∂x |eq    Ω = √(κ/m)    U = −∫ F dx

Force map over beam intensity (xz plane)

What am I looking at?

The colour is the intensity of the trapping light in the plane containing the beam axis; arrows show the direction of the total optical force on the sphere if its centre sat at that point (arrow length is normalised, so only the direction is shown). In a bright tweezer the arrows converge just behind the focus; in a dark (bottle-beam) trap they converge on the intensity minimum, because a resonant particle can be pushed out of the light.

Glossary
Optical tweezers / trap
A tightly focused laser beam that holds a small particle at (or near) its focus using the momentum of light.
Bright vs dark trap
In a bright trap the particle is pulled into the most intense light (true for most materials). Some resonant, high-index particles are instead pushed out of the light; they can be held in a dark trap — a pocket of darkness surrounded by light, such as the “bottle beam” made by overlapping two beams with opposite phase.
Gradient and scattering forces
The gradient force pulls the particle along intensity gradients (towards or away from bright regions); the scattering force (radiation pressure) pushes it along the beam. The equilibrium is where they balance, usually a little behind the focus.
Numerical aperture (NA)
How strongly the lens focuses: NA = n·sin(θ), where θ is the half-angle of the light cone. NA ≈ 0.9 is a tight focus (spot ≈ wavelength); smaller NA means a longer, wider, weaker focus.
Beam waist w₀
The radius of the focal spot (where the intensity drops to 1/e² ≈ 13% of the centre), an alternative to NA for describing the focus.
LG and HG modes
Laguerre–Gauss (LGₚₗ) and Hermite–Gauss (HGₘₙ) beams are shaped laser beams. LG beams with ℓ ≠ 0 are doughnuts carrying orbital angular momentum; p counts the bright rings. HG beams are grids of lobes. LG₀₀ = HG₀₀ is the ordinary Gaussian beam.
Polarisation
The direction in which the light’s electric field oscillates: linear (x or y) or circular (rotating). It slightly changes the trap shape, e.g. different stiffness along x and y.
Refractive index n + ik
n sets how strongly the particle bends light (glass ≈ 1.45, silicon ≈ 3.5); k is absorption (heats the particle). The medium index is 1 for vacuum or air.
Equilibrium zeq
Where the particle settles along the beam axis, measured from the focus (positive = downstream).
Stiffness κ and trap frequency f
Near equilibrium the force is like a spring, F = −κ·Δx. The particle (mass m) oscillates at f = √(κ/m)/2π, typically tens to hundreds of kHz. “Axial” is along the beam (z), “radial” across it (x, y).
Trap depth
The energy needed to escape the trap, in units of the thermal energy kBT (≈ 4×10⁻²¹ J at room temperature). Depths ≳ 10 kBT keep a particle trapped; in vacuum much more is needed during pump-down. Also shown as an equivalent temperature U/kB.
Multipoles (ED, MD, EQ, MQ, …)
The scattered light is a sum of simple radiation patterns: electric and magnetic dipoles (ED, MD), quadrupoles (EQ, MQ), octupoles (EO, MO) and so on. Small particles scatter mostly as an ED; larger or high-index particles have resonances in the higher ones, which can even reverse the force.
Dipole (Rayleigh) approximation
Treats the particle as a single point-like electric dipole. It is accurate for particles much smaller than the wavelength; comparing it with the full calculation shows when the simple picture breaks down.
Qsca, Qabs
Scattering and absorption efficiencies: the cross-section divided by the particle’s geometric cross-section πr², for a plane wave (textbook Mie values).
Recoil heating Γ/Ω
Every scattered photon gives the particle a random kick. Γ is the resulting heating rate in phonons per second, and Γ/Ω is the number of kicks’ worth of heating per oscillation (in radians). Γ/Ω ≪ 1 means the particle can oscillate coherently for many periods — important for quantum experiments.
Nmax
How many multipole orders are kept in the calculation, chosen automatically from the particle and beam size. Larger means more accurate and slower.
